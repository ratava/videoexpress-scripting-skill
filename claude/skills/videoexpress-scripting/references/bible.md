# The bible: character, outfit and location blocks

Read when writing or revising character, outfit or location blocks, start-image prompts, Consistent Character references, or non-human characters.

Write these once, paste them **word for word** into every image and video prompt that features them. Never paraphrase — paraphrase is drift. The one exception: a location block's out-of-frame sentence goes into video prompts only (below).

**Character core block** (identity, constant across scenes): age range, build, skin, face, eye colour, expression baseline, hair (cut, colour, how it's worn — use "always"), signature accessory (e.g. a hat, glasses or jewellery) with material, shape and how it's worn. Give the character a short unique name used in every prompt.
- **A closed-mouth expression baseline is good practice** ("a closed-mouth smile" rather than "mouth often open in a big smile"), and so is closing the mouth of anyone who isn't speaking first in a dialogue clip's start image. It's optional: in testing, open mouths didn't cause dropped or misattributed lines (the prompt's language did; `multishot-template.md`, Language).

**Outfit blocks** (one per scene): every garment with colour, material, cut, neckline, length, fastenings, and exactly which body parts are bare.
- **Describe asymmetric items per side, positively and separately.** "Her right wrist wears a slim silver watch. Her left wrist is bare." Adjacent mentions ("a watch and a bracelet on her wrists") bleed into each other.
- Prefer **snug, flat, close-fitting** wording for small accessories; "fluffy", "thick" or a second mention of the same item invites extra fabric.
- If the user prefers a look that emerged in a generation, rewrite the block to match that frame rather than fighting it.
- **Garment states: a suggestion when the user cares about one.** When the user has a requirement for how a garment is worn (zipped, buttoned, a hood up), suggest describing one finished state with no gap, and leave out what would show through a gap. In testing, "fully zipped from the hem to the top of the collar, the front one closed panel" closed a rain jacket in 9 of 9 images, where "zipped up to mid-chest, a T-shirt shows at the neck" came back fully open in all of about 23.
- **Small one-sided details can be ignored.** A single small asymmetric detail (hair tucked behind one ear so the ear shows) didn't render in about 28 images across VE and Artistly, whatever the wording or its place in the prompt. If the user's character has one and it matters to them, warn them it may not appear; if it doesn't matter to the story, offer to write the character the way the model draws it, so the bible matches the sheet.

**Location blocks** (one per environment): describe the whole space — floor, walls or railings, structures, skyline or ceiling, light sources, atmosphere, crowd — **including what is out of frame at the start**, so camera moves reveal defined space instead of invented space. Write the out-of-frame part as its own sentence ("Behind the camera at the start, out of frame: a shop counter with an old till.") and paste it into **video prompts only**. A start image has no off-screen: VE draws whatever the prompt names, so the out-of-frame items appear in the shot (in testing, 5 of 5 start images with the sentence, 0 of 5 without, across three locations). Start-image prompts take the rest of the block word for word. Add sky/weather direction rules here if clouds are present ("any clouds always travel directly away from the camera toward the horizon, never sideways").

**Crowd rule**: crowds need their own look distinct from the lead (different clothing), "a varied mix of people with different faces, hairstyles and builds", and "only [Name] wears [signature accessory]". For scale, say "every person a real person at true human scale with natural proportions", "the nearest people are the same size as [Name]", "the crowd recedes naturally with perspective". Avoid wording that invites mixed scales: a raised platform lifting the lead "high above", "thousands", "shrinking into tiny figures".

**Reference images vs the start image**: Consistent Character references (below) are for Create Image: they carry a character into start images. A clip gets only its start image, so a video prompt's [REFERENCE USE] names that image as the opening frame and nothing else; the bible blocks in the prompt carry the rest. Environment that a move will reveal is held by the location block, written in full including what is out of frame at the start.

**The Consistent Character check (ask every time).** Create Video ignores Use Consistent Character and both Reference Photo slots. A clip's characters come from its start image and the words of its prompt, and from nothing else. Two requests run against that, and a warning on its own has not stopped them:
1. **Consistent Character for a clip.** The user asks to tick it or fill its slots for Create Video, wants a clip planned around it, or says it is what put a character into a clip.
2. **A bible character who isn't in the start image appearing during the clip**: walking or flying in, revealed by a camera move, or entering in the last seconds to set up the next clip. No reference reaches that character. VE invents them from the prompt's words, so they differ from their sheet and from take to take.

Never plan either one yourself: every bible character in a clip is in that clip's start image. When the user asks for either, or wants a plan that depends on either, stop before writing the prompt or changing the pack, and each time:
- Say in one or two sentences, in the user's language, what VE will do (above).
- Link the training video: [Consistent Character in VideoExpress](https://youtu.be/CVeYOk7Rkf0).
- Ask one question, as a question card (the platform's question tool where there is one; in plain text with the two options numbered where there isn't), with exactly two options, translated into the user's language: **"I am aware, just do it"** and **"Let's change the approach"**.

Ask again every time it comes up, including when they said "I am aware" earlier in the project: the answer covers that one clip or request, never the rest of the pack.
- **"I am aware, just do it":** write what was asked. Put the character's full bible block in the prompt, since the words are all VE has, and note in the pack, beside that clip, that the character is invented by VE there, not held by a reference.
- **"Let's change the approach":** put the character in the start image. Make a new start image in Create Image with Consistent Character on and the character's reference in a slot (two reference characters at most), composed for the beat with the character already in frame, and start the clip fresh from that image, joined to the previous clip by a cut. A character who is already in frame can then move, come closer or speak.

**A result doesn't change the rule.** When the user reports that Consistent Character "worked" in a clip, or that a character only appeared because it was ticked, the character came from the start image or from the prompt's description. Don't rewrite the bible, the pack or the plan to say Consistent Character works in video; run the check.

**Consistent Character references (VE 3.5)**:
- Reference Photo (slot 1) holds the main character; Reference Photo 2 holds a second character, or an empty location (the set with no characters) to hold its layout across scenes. Both are picked from the library (Media Library → My AI Images), so build them first as clean full-length reference images on a plain grey background, or, for a detailed character, as a composite sheet (below).
- **A reference can carry its pose and props.** A reference in either slot can bring its pose and props into the result (e.g. a weapon held in the hands when the prompt says slung), though it doesn't always. A pose stated in the prompt overrides the reference's pose, so state the pose and how each prop is held. Use slot 2 for a second character's reference sheet, not for an in-scene frame used as a style reference.
- **Turn Consistent Character off for start images with no characters** (empty environments, point-of-view shots, insert shots). With it on, VE puts the reference character in the image. It affects Create Image only; Create Video ignores it.
- VE rewrites the image prompt before generating only when **Automatically enhance my image prompt** is ticked, with or without Consistent Character. Keep it off; if it was on, read the image prompt box after generation and report the rewrite.
- **A reference's stance can carry into the start image.** With a full-length reference in slot 1, a mild pose instruction ("turned slightly toward the left") was overridden by the reference's square-on stance in every take. When a start image needs a turn or movement, put the character mid-action (mid-stride, mid-turn) so the pose has more pull than the reference.

**The reference sheet standard.** A Consistent Character reference carries more than a face: its pose, props, expression and any small detail can carry into every start image made from it. A reference meets the standard when:
1. **One character alone**, the whole body in frame from head to feet (or to the tips of the wings, tail or limbs), nothing else in frame except a scale object (point 10).
2. **A plain, light grey background**, no scenery, floor detail or props lying around.
3. **A calm pose the body allows** (guidance, not a hard rule): for a human or humanoid, relaxed and standing three-quarter or front-on, hands empty and away from the body, feet visible. Many animal characters can't do that: a flying insect hovers with its wings spread and its limbs out, a fish or a snake has no stance at all. Judge the pose against the character's own body, and treat it as a miss only when a calmer pose was possible and the one shown is dramatic or mid-action (hands on hips, mid-leap, a fighting stance), because that pose carries into start images and fights the pose the prompt asks for.
4. **A neutral, closed-mouth expression** (good practice, not required): an open mouth or a big grin can carry into start images and into the clips made from them.
5. **No small text or logos on the body**: small badges, patches and printed marks smear and change; describe them as plain shapes or leave them off. Labels beside the figure aren't needed (VE doesn't copy them).
6. **Every design detail the bible names is visible and matches it**: outfit, colours, props on the correct side. A detail the sheet doesn't show is invented from the prompt.
7. **For a detailed character, a composite**: full body, a large head shot and key props, as below. Back and side views are optional but help, especially with hair.
8. **A new outfit doesn't need a new sheet.** The sheet carries the character (face, skin, hair, build, signature accessory); the start-image prompt sets the clothes. Write the new outfit block in full, every garment down to the footwear, since anything the prompt leaves open can come from the sheet. In testing, a sheet in a jumper and trousers, with a parka outfit in the prompt, gave the parka as well as a parka sheet did (identity held in 3 of 3, no jumper or trousers, the same outfit score), and a strapless red evening gown came out in 3 of 3; black shoes showed under the gown in 2 of 3, where the prompt named no footwear. Make a sheet for an outfit (from the first, neutral sheet, only the clothing changed) when a garment's small details must match from shot to shot: with the outfit's own sheet in the slot, VE drew the same coat in every take, while from the prompt alone its smaller details (collar, hood) changed between takes. Make one sheet per prop state (sheathed and armed, say).
9. **Clean**: no artefacts, extra fingers or merged parts, since VE copies those too.
10. **Scale shown where possible** (guidance, not a hard rule): a reference alone on grey says nothing about size, so VE sizes the character from the prompt's words, and a small creature can come out monster-sized. Where the sheet allows it, show the character beside one plain, familiar object of known size (an acorn beside an insect, a plain wooden chair beside a person-sized character), standing apart from the character so it isn't read as a prop the character holds or wears; this is the one exception to "nothing else in frame". **The props set the scale between characters.** When two characters with size props share a start image, VE sizes them to the ratio of their props, and a size comparison in the image prompt is ignored (in testing, a fox sheet with a kitchen chair and a firefly sheet with an acorn gave a firefly about the size of the fox's head in 2 of 2 candidates; the prompt's "smaller than his paw" was ignored in both). So choose each character's prop for the size you want relative to the others, and judge the result by the props' ratio. **Keep the props out of the scene:** in the start-image prompt, say positively what the only things in the scene are, and name the prop in the clip's [NEGATIVES] (0 props in 6 of 6 candidates in that test). **Ask the size, and explain the prop, before writing the sheet.** A user who sees a chair or an acorn on a character sheet can take it for a mistake, and the size of a character is the user's decision, never a guess. So whenever you write a reference-sheet prompt (a new sheet or a regeneration), first ask, in the user's language, how big the character is, with an everyday comparison as the answer ("about as tall as a kitchen chair", "small enough to sit in a hand", "the size of a fingertip"); with two or more characters, ask how big they are next to each other. Then tell the user, once per project, what the prop is for: "I'll put [object] beside [character] on the sheet as a size guide. It isn't part of the scene and won't appear in your images; it tells VideoExpress how big [character] is, and next to other characters' guides, how big they are compared with each other." Name the prop and the relationship you will draw ("the chair seat reaches the middle of his thighs"), and let the user correct it before you write the prompt. Note the chosen sizes in the bible beside each character. Where a prop doesn't fit the sheet, the size lives in the character block instead: give it by comparison ("its body the length of a fingertip", "it stands as tall as a kitchen chair"), never by numbers alone, since VE has no sense of what a centimetre figure means.

**References the user brings: inspect before use.** When the user supplies reference images or sheets they made themselves (in another image tool, in VE outside this pack, or anywhere else), never take them on trust. Before any prompt or plan uses them:
- **Tell the user you are checking them against the standard**, then look at each one at full size and check every point above.
- **If one doesn't meet it, say so, and why**: each point it misses, in the user's language, with what that will do in VE ("his hands-on-hips pose will carry into the start images"; "the small blank badge on his jacket will smear"). Never note a problem only in the pack, a memory note or your own reasoning: if you saw it, the user hears it before the reference is used.
- **Ask what to do** (question card where there is one, numbered options where there isn't), translated into the user's language: **"Use it as it is"** or **"Regenerate it"**. On regenerate, offer the routes the user has: their own image tool (the one they made it in), VE Create Image, or Artistly if they own it (SKILL.md, Companion products). Write an all-positive reference prompt in English that fixes every miss, for whichever route they choose; for their own tool, the prompt may need adapting to it.
- **Record the outcome in the pack** beside each reference: checked, what it misses, and the user's choice. Ask once per reference; ask again only if the reference changes or a new problem shows up in a take.

A reference that meets the standard is noted as checked, with a one-line "meets the standard" to the user.

**Sheets made in the project are checked too.** Every reference sheet VE (or another tool) produces for the project, regenerations included, is checked against the standard and the character's bible block at full size before it goes in a slot: views, defining features, props and small details. A regeneration that fixes one point can break another (in testing: a lost wing; then a dropped glow, a standing figure and a doubled front view; a second badge and invented lettering on a bag tag). Tell the user what the candidate misses before they pick it.

**Detailed character reference sheets: build them in stages.** VE's image model loses features buried in the middle of a long prompt: in testing, a disfigured cyborg face dropped its plate, lens and scar in prompts of about four thousand characters, and the skin went grey, while the same face held in prompts under about fifteen hundred characters with the face described first (see also the advised lengths in `prompt-rules.md`, Prompt length and format). Long, detailed prompts still help a style hold; they don't help a handful of defining features survive. So when the user wants a highly detailed character, don't put everything in one prompt:
1. **Head shot first.** A short prompt: a one-line style anchor, then the face and its defining features, then little else. Iterate until the face is right.
2. **If the design won't hold from words,** take the best head shot VE has produced (iterate on it until it is right), put it in slot 1 and regenerate from it. The reference carries the design and VE redraws it in its own style; the prompt wording barely matters for what the reference shows. Clean any artefacts out of the reference first, because VE copies them too. Only if VE consistently fails to produce the design after iteration, offer the user the option of making the reference image in another image tool of their choice and importing it to the library; don't name a tool.
3. **Full body with the head shot in slot 1.** A short prompt that spends its words on body, outfit and props; the reference carries the face.
4. **Composite sheet.** Assemble one image: full body on the left third, head shot large at top right, key props at bottom right, plain background, no text labels. Make one sheet per prop state (sheathed and armed, say) and choose by scene. The composite goes in slot 1 and leaves slot 2 free for a second character. Before production, test it once with a simple start image: in testing it gave exactly one figure with no panels or layout bleed in every take.

**Match prompts to the image that worked.** When a start-image prompt produces the look you want, reuse its exact character, outfit and location wording in the video prompts for that sequence, rather than the bible's older wording. A mismatch between the frame and the text (different hair side, garment names, a feature that isn't drawn) makes the video drift toward the text.

**Props: describe exactly how the frame shows them.** If the start image shows a bag hanging from one shoulder and resting at the hip, write that, not "a backpack on her back". The model reconciles the frame and the text by moving the prop, often mid-clip.

**Non-human and mechanical characters**:
- **Humanoid designs generate far more reliably** than multi-legged or unusual body plans. Leg and limb counts are ignored, and bodies with many thin limbs lose consistency between frames. Prefer two arms and two legs with a distinctive head, silhouette and weapon.
- **Describe complex limbs and weapons by structure, joint by joint**, in the order they attach: "a rounded shoulder plate over an armoured upper arm and a large cylindrical elbow joint; from the elbow down there is no forearm and no hand, the forearm is the weapon itself, a boxy housing leading into a cluster of barrels". Naming the part ("arm cannon", "rotary gun") alone lets the model draw a hand holding a gun.
- **Mechanical limbs revert to hands during gestures.** When a character with a weapon-arm yells, points or raises an arm, the weapon can morph into a fist. Give gestures to the other limb ("raising its left fist while its right-arm weapon stays pointed at the ground") and add "the weapon keeps exactly the same shape, never turning into a hand or fist".
- VE adds generic gun furniture (carry handles, grips, top rails). Negate it explicitly: "nothing mounted on top: no carry handle, no top handle, no grip".
- **Hybrid props collapse into their stronger half.** A "sword-pistol" or "jutte-pistol" was drawn by VE as a handgun with a blade attached; the gun absorbed the design. Describe the parts and their layout as plain geometry ("two parallel prongs joined at the base", "a boxy black block above the rear half of the blade", "a grip in line with the blade like a sword handle") and keep the stronger noun out of the prompt. Even then, a hybrid that leaves the frame can come back as its stronger half (see camera-and-motion.md); if it keeps reverting, simplify the design. A plain, familiar prop drifts least.

**Start images**: an image prompt carries **no bracketed pack reference**. The pack declares the reference in the image's heading (Image 12A) and the prompt starts straight with its content. VE paints a bracketed reference at the front of an image prompt onto any surface it was going to letter anyway: "3A" on a bakery's sign board, a character's name on a shop sign, text on a shirt (in testing, 3 of 7 tagged signage images, 0 of 6 untagged). Rename the saved tile to its reference instead (`browser-workflow.md`). Write each image prompt as a **self-contained** prompt with the full character and outfit blocks and the location block (without its out-of-frame sentence) inlined — no "derived from reference X" split prompts. Put the subject **mid-action** if the clip must start moving (a still pose in the start frame makes the model hesitate). Always include the character's head. Derive later start images from the best frame of the character, never from a drifted frame.

**Image prompts carry no negatives.** Start images and reference sheets have no [NEGATIVES] section and no "no …" phrases: everything in an image prompt is read as something to draw. State each exclusion as the positive it stands for, in the section it belongs to: "the rest of the terrace empty", "her hands empty at her sides", "a blank, unlettered sign board", "smooth matte painted surfaces". Negatives stay in video prompts, where [NEGATIVES] works.

**Tagged start-image prompts.** A start-image prompt can use section tags instead of prose, in the same spirit as the video prompt: `<medium sentence> [REFERENCE USE] … [SUBJECT] … [FRAMING AND POSITION] … [BACKGROUND] … [LIGHT AND STYLE] …`. **The style's medium sentence comes first, before any tag** (the sheet's medium phrase: "A beautifully crafted claymation stop-motion film still, widescreen 16:9."), as in the prose recipe (`styles/README.md`). With it only at the end, in [LIGHT AND STYLE], a Claymation start image came out near-photoreal; moved to the front, the same prompt gave clay. Tested against the same content written as prose: subject, held props, framing, background layout and light direction held as well, and no tag text appeared in the image. [SUBJECT] holds the character and outfit blocks and the pose; [FRAMING AND POSITION] the shot size and where the subject sits in the frame; [BACKGROUND] the location block, with anything that must stay empty or blank said positively; [LIGHT AND STYLE] the light and the style's closing look line (its materials and rendering), restating the medium. There is no [NEGATIVES] section. Leave out [REFERENCE USE] when no reference slot is used. Either form works; keep one form throughout a pack.
